# Synthetic Data

> **Term status — Established.** A recognized term of art, in independent use beyond any single originator.

## One-line essence
Data generated by AI rather than collected from the real world — useful when real data is scarce, sensitive, or legally restricted, with its own quality and bias risks.

---

## Technical definition

Synthetic data is data produced by a generative process — increasingly a model — rather than recorded from observation. It is used to fill gaps in real corpora, to cover rare cases, to stand in for data that is legally or ethically restricted, and to reduce the cost of annotation.

**The case for it is real and should not be understated.** Liu et al. (2024) survey it as a response to data scarcity, privacy constraints and acquisition cost, with demonstrated empirical effectiveness — and set three conditions on responsible use: **factuality, unbiasedness, and fidelity**. Those are not caveats appended to a recommendation; they are the load-bearing part of it.

**The most important limit is a feedback effect, and it is measured.** Shumailov et al. (*Nature*, 2024) demonstrate *model collapse*: when models are trained on data produced by earlier models, across generations the tails of the distribution disappear first and the model converges toward a narrowed version of reality. **The degradation is not random noise — it is systematic loss of the rare and the unusual**, which is precisely the part of a distribution that governance, safety and fairness work depends on. A model trained this way can look fine on aggregate metrics while having lost the cases that mattered.

**A synthetic corpus inherits its generator's biases and cannot correct them.** If the generating model under-represents a group, so does everything it produces — but the output now arrives with the appearance of a fresh, independent dataset. **Synthetic data launders provenance**: it converts a known limitation into an unmarked one unless the lineage is deliberately recorded ([data provenance and lineage](data-provenance-lineage.md)).

**It does not automatically solve the privacy problem it is often bought for.** Data generated by a model trained on personal records is not thereby free of them; models memorize and can emit training content ([data leakage](data-leakage-ai-systems.md)). Synthetic generation reduces re-identification risk in some designs and merely obscures it in others, and the difference is a property of the method, not of the label.

**And it is a direct route to evaluation contamination.** If synthetic training data is generated by a model that has seen your benchmarks, or if synthetic test cases resemble training content, measured performance rises without capability rising ([data leakage in model evaluation](data-leakage-model-evaluation.md)).

**Regulatory footing exists.** The EU AI Act's Article 10 data-governance obligations for high-risk systems — representativeness, relevance, error examination — apply to the training data regardless of whether a human or a model produced it. Synthetic origin is not an exemption.

## Plain-language version

Synthetic data is information a computer made up rather than collected. That sounds like cheating, and sometimes it is exactly the right move: when real records are private, when the situation you need examples of is rare, when collecting and labeling the real thing would cost more than the project is worth.

The trouble arrives when it feeds itself. Researchers trained models on the output of earlier models, generation after generation, and found something they called model collapse. The unusual cases go first. Each round, the rare examples thin out, and the model drifts toward a blander, narrower picture of the world — while still looking healthy by the usual measures. The parts that vanish are the edge cases, which are the parts that fairness and safety work actually cares about.

There is a second problem that is easier to miss. Made-up data inherits whatever was wrong with the model that made it, but it arrives looking brand new. A dataset with a known gap is a manageable problem. A generated dataset with the same gap and no label on it is a hidden one.

And it does not automatically make privacy go away. Data generated by a model that learned from real people's records is not thereby clean of those records. Sometimes the method genuinely protects people. Sometimes it just makes the risk harder to see. Which one you have depends on how it was built, not on what it is called.

Used deliberately — to cover a specific gap, with its origin written down, checked against real data — synthetic data is a good tool. Used to make a corpus bigger because bigger seemed better, it degrades quietly.

## AI literacy notes

1. **Synthetic data is a legitimate technique with real uses** — scarcity, privacy, cost, rare-case coverage — not a shortcut to be avoided on principle.
2. **Recursive training degrades systematically, not randomly.** The rare cases disappear first, which is the opposite of what you would want to lose.
3. **Aggregate metrics hide collapse**, because what is lost is in the tails rather than the average.
4. **A generator's biases pass straight through**, and arrive looking like an independent dataset.
5. **Synthetic origin launders provenance unless it is recorded** — the limitation stops being visible without being fixed.
6. **"Synthetic" does not mean "private."** Whether it protects individuals depends on the generation method, not the label.
7. **It is a fast route to benchmark contamination**, inflating measured performance without capability changing.
8. **Regulatory data-governance duties do not lift** because a model produced the data rather than a person.

## Governance notes

**Core question:** Which parts of this training or evaluation corpus were generated rather than observed, what produced them, and what real-world data have we checked them against?

**Watch for:**
- No record of which records are synthetic, so the proportion is unknown and unrecoverable ([data provenance and lineage](data-provenance-lineage.md))
- Successive model generations trained on predecessors' output with no fresh real data entering the pipeline
- Corpus growth reported as a quality improvement without any check on distribution coverage
- Rare-case, minority-group or edge-case performance untested, where collapse shows up first ([bias](bias-ai-systems.md))
- Synthetic data adopted as a privacy control without an assessment of the specific generation method ([privacy](privacy-ai-systems.md))
- Evaluation sets generated by, or resembling the output of, the model under test ([data leakage in model evaluation](data-leakage-model-evaluation.md))
- Vendor-supplied datasets whose synthetic proportion is undisclosed
- High-risk systems where Article 10 representativeness duties are assumed not to apply to generated data

**Practice:**
- **Label synthetic records at creation and carry that label through every downstream use** — retrofitting provenance is not possible
- Record the generating model, version, prompt or configuration alongside the data ([model version and update](model-version-update.md))
- **Keep real data in the loop** and measure coverage of tails and rare classes explicitly, not only aggregate quality
- Set and monitor the synthetic proportion of any corpus as a governed figure with a ceiling
- Evaluate against held-out *real* data; never let synthetic generation touch the test set
- Treat a privacy claim as requiring evidence about the method — memorization and re-identification risk must be tested, not assumed away
- Document synthetic components in dataset documentation and model reporting ([model card / system card](model-card-system-card.md))
- Verify the representativeness duties in [compliance](compliance-ai-systems.md) scope apply equally to generated data

**Key accountability owner:** whoever owns the training corpus — because the risk is not that synthetic data is used, but that its proportion and origin stop being recorded, and after that no one can answer what the model actually learned from.

## Confidence level

**High on the risks, medium on the practice.** Model collapse is demonstrated in a peer-reviewed *Nature* paper, and the responsible-use conditions come from a substantial survey; the regulatory position follows directly from the text of the obligations. **What is not settled is where the safe boundary lies.** No agreed threshold exists for what proportion of a corpus may be synthetic, no standard method for detecting synthetic content in a dataset you did not build, and no consensus on which generation methods deliver genuine privacy protection versus apparent protection. Collapse was demonstrated under repeated recursive training; **how strongly it applies to real pipelines that mix synthetic with fresh real data is an open question, and this entry does not claim otherwise.** Treat the direction as established and any specific ratio or safety claim — including a vendor's — as unproven.
