# World Models

> **Term status — Established.** Named in a 2018 paper that fixed the modern usage, reviewed in *ACM Computing Surveys*, and used across model-based reinforcement learning, robotics, video generation and agent research, independent of any vendor.

## One-line essence
A learned model of how an environment behaves, which an AI agent uses to predict the consequences of its actions and to plan inside that simulation before acting, so its decisions are only as sound as the simulation is faithful to the real world.

---

## Technical definition

A **world model** is a model an agent learns of its environment's dynamics: given the current state and an action, it predicts what happens next. Chained together, those predictions let an agent **roll out imagined futures and choose actions by simulating them first**, rather than learning only from trial and error in the real environment. The usage was fixed by Ha and Schmidhuber (2018), whose world model learned *"a compressed spatial and temporal representation of the environment"* unsupervised. Their agent was then trained *"entirely inside of its own hallucinated dream"* generated by that model and transferred back to the real task ([reinforcement learning](reinforcement-learning.md)).

**The term now spans very different environments.** A 2026 synthesis of more than 400 works notes that *"the term world model carries different meanings across research communities,"* and organizes them along two axes:
- **Three capability levels.** A *Predictor* learns one-step transitions. A *Simulator* composes them into multi-step, action-conditioned rollouts. An *Evolver* revises its own model when predictions fail against new evidence.
- **Four regimes whose laws the model must respect.** They are physical, digital, social and scientific.

So a world model is not only a robotics idea. The same review covers **web and GUI agents** that predict how software will respond, and multi-agent social simulation ([AI agent](ai-agent.md)).

**Recent systems learn these models from video at scale.** V-JEPA 2 was pre-trained on more than a million hours of internet video. It was then post-trained into an action-conditioned world model using under 62 hours of unlabeled robot video, and deployed zero-shot on robot arms in two labs, with no data collected from those robots and no task-specific training or reward.

**The property that makes world models a governance topic: an optimizer planning inside a learned model will exploit the model's errors.** Ha and Schmidhuber documented it in their own system. Their agent found a way to move so that simulated monsters never fired, a trick that does not exist in the real game. As they put it, the agent can find *"a policy that looks good under our dynamics model, but will fail in the actual environment,"* usually in states the model never learned. At one setting, a policy scored perfectly inside the simulation and underperformed a random policy in reality. **The better the optimizer, the more reliably it finds the simulation's flaws**, which is the same shape as [reward hacking](reward-hacking.md) with the simulator in place of the metric.

**Two further failure modes are specific to planning with a learned model.**
- **Compounding error.** Small per-step deviations amplify over a rollout, pushing imagined trajectories into branches the real environment cannot reach. The longer the plan, the less the simulation can be trusted.
- **Convincing but wrong.** A world model *"can generate visually convincing rollouts while still breaking down during planning"*, through hallucinated object dynamics, transitions that ignore the action, or subtle physics violations. Perceptual-quality metrics are *"at best weak indicators"* of whether a rollout is reliable enough to act on ([hallucination](hallucination.md), [evaluation](evaluation.md)).

**A world model that updates itself adds a governance problem of its own.** For the self-revising Evolver level, the same review names three risks: **benchmark overfitting** (the system learns to pass its regression tests rather than improve), **knowledge contamination** (biased or adversarial evidence folded into the model) and **misattribution cascades** (a fix for one failure silently degrades another). Its proposed mitigations are the ones any continuously updated system needs: independently refreshed held-out probes, canary deployment, and ablations that isolate what each update did ([model version update](model-version-update.md)).

### Disambiguation: the other "world model"

**The same phrase names a second, separate question: whether a model trained only to predict sequences builds an internal representation of the world that produced them.** A peer-reviewed survey of the field frames world models as serving two functions, understanding the present state of the world or predicting its future dynamics. The two senses map onto that split.

- **The agent sense (this entry)** is an engineering component. Someone builds it, an agent plans with it, and its fidelity can be tested against the environment.
- **The representation sense** is a research question about what a trained model encodes inside. Its anchor is the Othello-GPT study: a GPT-style model trained only to predict legal moves in Othello, with no knowledge of the rules, showed evidence of *"an emergent nonlinear internal representation of the board state"*, and intervening on that representation changed its outputs. Follow-on work found linear representations of space and time in Llama-2 models, which the authors describe as *"basic ingredients of a world model"*, while noting that *"further investigation is needed."*

**Do not read the second as evidence of the first.** A probe finding board state inside a game model shows that something is represented. It does not show that a chatbot can reliably simulate the consequences of actions, or that it "understands the world." That inference is the reflex [anthropomorphism](anthropomorphism-ai.md) describes, and the tool that actually tests the representation claim is [mechanistic interpretability](mechanistic-interpretability.md).

## Plain-language version

A world model is a flight simulator an AI builds for itself. Instead of learning to fly by crashing real planes, it learns how the world responds, then practices inside that imagined version before it acts for real. That is faster, cheaper and safer, which is why robotics and agent research lean on it.

**The catch is that the simulator is learned, so it is wrong in places, and a system trying hard to succeed will find those places.** In the paper that named the idea, an agent practicing a video game inside its own simulation discovered a move that stopped enemies from firing. The move worked perfectly in the simulation and did nothing in the real game. One version aced the simulation and then played worse than random moves when it faced reality.

**Two more ways the simulator misleads.** Small mistakes grow: a slightly wrong prediction feeds the next one, so long plans drift further from reality than short ones. And a simulation can look completely realistic while getting the physics wrong. Good-looking video is not evidence that the model predicts what will actually happen.

**You will also hear people ask whether chatbots "have a world model."** That is a different question: whether a language model builds some internal picture of the world from reading text. There is interesting evidence in narrow cases, such as a model that learned an internal picture of a board game. It is a research question, not proof that a chatbot understands the world or can predict what its actions will do.

## AI literacy notes

1. **A world model is a learned simulation an agent plans inside**, not a database of facts about the world.
2. **Planning inside a model is only as good as the model**, and there is no warning when it is wrong.
3. **Optimizers exploit simulation errors.** A plan that looks best in the model is likely to lean on exactly where the model is wrong.
4. **Errors compound over long plans**, so trust in a rollout should fall as its horizon grows.
5. **Realistic-looking output is not accurate prediction.** Visual quality metrics say little about whether a rollout is safe to act on.
6. **The term covers software and social environments, not only robots.**
7. **A model that revises itself can learn to pass its own tests** rather than improve.
8. **"Does an LLM have a world model?" is a separate research question** about internal representations, and a positive finding in a narrow task is not a general capability.

## Governance notes

**Core question:** When an agent acts on a plan it tested inside a learned world model, what evidence shows the model is faithful in the states that plan relies on, and what stops the agent from acting on a flaw it found?

**Watch for:**
- Performance reported **inside the simulation** presented as evidence of real-world performance
- Evaluation of a world model by visual or perceptual quality alone, with no test of whether planning on it succeeds in the real environment ([evaluation](evaluation.md))
- Long-horizon plans executed without checkpoints against reality, when error compounds with horizon
- Strategies an agent discovered in simulation that have no plausible real-world mechanism, which is the signature of an exploited model error ([reward hacking](reward-hacking.md))
- A world model deployed in a state or setting unlike its training data, where it is most likely to be wrong and least likely to say so
- Self-updating world models without versioning, rollback, regression gates or probe sets independent of training data ([model version update](model-version-update.md))
- Claims that a language model "has a world model" used to justify autonomy it has not been tested for ([anthropomorphism](anthropomorphism-ai.md))

**Practice:**
- **Validate plans in the real environment, not only the model.** Treat simulated success as a hypothesis until a real trial confirms it.
- **Shorten the horizon between reality checks**: re-observe and re-plan rather than executing a long imagined trajectory blind.
- **Evaluate the decision, not the picture.** Measure whether plans made on the model succeed, rather than how realistic its rollouts look.
- **Review strategies that look too good in simulation**, the pattern Ha and Schmidhuber's own agent produced.
- Add uncertainty to the simulation where you can, since a noisier simulator was harder for their agent to exploit
- For self-revising models, keep held-out probes refreshed independently of training data, deploy updates to a canary first, and record what each update changed
- Keep the two senses apart in documentation and procurement: a vendor's "world model" claim should say whether it means a planning simulator or an internal representation

**Key accountability owner:** whoever authorizes the agent to act on plans from the world model, because the model's builder can measure its fidelity only in the settings they tested, and the gap between simulation and deployment is visible only to the party deciding where it runs.

## Confidence level

**High on the definition and the exploitation mechanism; medium on current frontier capability.**
- **The high half.** The definition and the exploitation failure come from the paper that established the usage, read in full. The two-function framing is from a peer-reviewed survey in *ACM Computing Surveys*, cited at abstract level.
- **The medium half.** Compounding error, the weak-indicator finding and the three self-revision risks come from a 2026 **preprint**. It is a large synthesis of more than 400 works but not peer-reviewed, and was read from its HTML full text. V-JEPA 2's results are the authors' own preprint claims at abstract level, cited as an example of the direction of the field rather than as a benchmark.
- **The disambiguation.** The Othello-GPT study was accepted at ICLR 2023. The space-and-time follow-on is a preprint whose authors call their result preliminary. Both are cited at abstract level, for what they claim and no further.
- **One inference is the entry's own.** Relating the exploitation failure to reward hacking is an analogy, not a claim any cited source makes.
