# Performativity (LLMs)

> **Term status — Emerging.** In active use and genuinely useful, but not yet settled — definitions still vary between sources. Read the Confidence level before relying on the term in a formal document.

## One-line essence
The way language models actively shape what people believe, say, and expect — not just reflecting language, but influencing it at scale.

---

## Technical definition

**Performativity** comes from Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* (1962): the observation that some utterances do not describe a state of affairs but **enact** one. Saying "I promise" is not a report about a promise; it is the promise. The general point is that language is an instrument of change, not only of description.

Applied to predictive systems, the idea has a formal statement. Perdomo et al. (ICML 2020) define **performative prediction** as the case where *"predictions support decisions they may influence the outcome they aim to predict."* The forecast becomes part of the causal history of the thing forecast. Their consequence matters for evaluation: a model can be accurate against historical data and wrong about the world it is now helping to create, so they propose **performative stability** — predictions that hold up against the outcomes that follow from acting on them — in place of calibration against the past.

**For LLMs the effect has now been measured, and this is what moves the term from theory to governance.** Yakura et al. found that words preferentially generated by ChatGPT — *delve, showcase, boast, intricacies, meticulous* — **increased abruptly in spontaneous human speech.** A synthetic-control analysis of **737,083 hours of conversation from 824,634 podcast episodes**, screened for unscripted speech, *"causally links this shift to ChatGPT's release."*

Because a natural experiment can always be questioned, the same work adds a **preregistered experiment (N = 496)**: a brief chatbot interaction *"led participants to adopt its words as their own, persisting past a distractor task and confirmed in forced lexical choice, indicating entrenchment in the active vocabulary."* Population-level correlation with a causal design, plus individual-level adoption under controlled conditions — the two together are what make this hard to dismiss.

**The loop closes.** Models trained on human text now *"feed their own traits back into human language, integrating LLMs into the ongoing processes of cultural evolution."* That output becomes the input to later training ([training data](training-data.md)), so the influence is recursive rather than one-off.

**The authors name the governance concern directly**, and it is not about any single deployment: *"linguistic homogenization and the capacity of a few major AI providers for latent cultural influence at scale."* The distinctive property is that **the harm is aggregate and the decisions are local.** No organization's use of an LLM causes measurable homogenization, and all of them together do.

**Distinguish it from two neighbors that are frequently conflated.** [Sycophancy](sycophancy-llms.md) is the model accommodating the user within a response. [Anthropomorphism](anthropomorphism-ai.md) is the user attributing human qualities to the model. **Performativity runs in the opposite direction from both: the model changing the user**, durably, and often below notice.

## Plain-language version

We tend to assume an AI model is a mirror — it learned from how we write, so it gives back how we write. The direction of influence is the other way round more than people expect.

Researchers looked at what happened to human speech after ChatGPT launched. Words the model happens to favor — *delve*, *showcase*, *boast*, *intricacies*, *meticulous* — started showing up noticeably more often in ordinary, unscripted talking. Not writing, where you might be using the tool directly. **Talking.** The analysis covered over 700,000 hours of podcast conversation and links the shift to the model's release.

They then checked it a second way, in a controlled experiment with about 500 people. A short chat with a bot was enough for participants to start using its words as their own — and it stuck, even after being distracted with an unrelated task.

So the pattern is: the model learned from us, and now we are learning from it. That loop keeps going, because what people write next becomes what future models are trained on.

What is worth worrying about here is not any one conversation. It is the aggregate. If a few companies' models are quietly nudging everyone's vocabulary in the same direction, language gets more uniform — and the nudging is not something anyone voted for or can easily see happening.

## AI literacy notes

1. **Influence runs both ways, and the return path is measured.** The model is not only a product of human language; it is now an input to it.
2. **The effect reaches spontaneous speech**, not just writing done with the tool open — which is why "just don't use it for that" is not a containment strategy.
3. **Adoption persists.** In the controlled experiment it survived a distractor task and showed up in forced lexical choice, indicating entrenchment rather than momentary priming.
4. **The concern is homogenization, not any single word.** Many people drawing on a few models converges style and vocabulary.
5. **The loop is recursive.** Model output becomes tomorrow's training data, so influence compounds instead of decaying.
6. **The harm is aggregate; the decisions are local.** No individual deployment causes it, which is exactly why nobody's risk assessment catches it.
7. **Not sycophancy, not anthropomorphism.** Those are the model accommodating you and you humanizing the model. This is the model changing you.
8. **Accuracy against the past can mislead** when a system helps produce the future it is predicting — the performative-prediction problem.

## Governance notes

**Core question:** Where our systems shape how people write, speak or decide, what is that influence doing in aggregate — and would we be able to tell if it were doing something we would not choose?

**Watch for:**
- A model's default voice becoming the organization's voice by accretion, through templates and drafts nobody decided to standardize on
- House style, tone and terminology converging on a vendor's defaults with no decision recorded ([shadow AI](shadow-ai.md))
- Model-drafted text feeding back into the corpora, guidance and [knowledge bases](knowledge-base.md) that later systems draw on
- Recommendation or scoring systems where the output influences the behavior being scored, then evaluated against historical data ([evaluation](evaluation.md))
- Aggregate or cultural effects absent from risk assessment because no single deployment is material
- Single-vendor dependence across all language-facing functions, concentrating whatever influence exists ([systemic risk](systemic-risk-ai.md))
- Model-generated text entering datasets unlabeled, so the loop cannot be traced ([data provenance](data-provenance-lineage.md))
- Distinctive institutional or professional vocabulary quietly flattening into generic register

**Practice:**
- **Label model-generated text where it enters durable corpora**, so the feedback loop is at least traceable ([content provenance](content-provenance-watermarking.md))
- Where a system's output influences the behavior it predicts, evaluate against outcomes that follow from deployment — not against historical calibration alone
- Treat house voice and domain terminology as things to specify deliberately, rather than letting a default fill the vacuum
- Keep human-authored reference material for domains where precision of language carries legal or clinical weight
- Name aggregate and cultural effects in risk assessment even though they are immaterial per deployment — the alternative is a class of effect nobody owns
- Prefer diversity of models and drafting practice where language uniformity is itself a risk
- Watch for professional vocabulary loss as a signal of [deskilling](cognitive-offloading-deskilling.md), not merely of style change

**Key accountability owner:** whoever owns the organization's language and knowledge assets — communications, editorial, or knowledge management — because this is a slow drift in institutional voice and vocabulary, and it will not appear as an incident, a metric, or anything an AI risk owner is looking for.

## Confidence level

**Medium-High for what has actually been measured, and lower for the scope the term implies — the gap matters.** The lexical finding is unusually well supported: a synthetic-control design over a very large corpus of unscripted speech, *plus* a preregistered experiment showing individual adoption and persistence. Convergent evidence from two different designs is strong.

**But the evidence is about vocabulary.** The one-line essence says these systems shape what people *believe, say, and expect*; the measured result covers **say** — specific word choices — and does not establish the other two. Whether lexical influence extends to beliefs, reasoning or expectations is plausible, actively studied, and **not demonstrated here.** Read the entry accordingly, and do not cite the podcast study for claims about belief.

Two further limits. The work is recent and its central corpus is English-language podcast speech, so generalization across languages and settings is untested. And **homogenization is the authors' stated concern rather than their measured finding** — the convergence hypothesis follows from the mechanism but has not itself been quantified.
